\section{Method}
Let \(\epsilon_\theta\) denote a pretrained and frozen diffusion model, and let \(\phi\) denote the parameters of a low-rank adaptation applied to its denoising network. The adapted predictor is

\[
\epsilon_{\theta,\phi}(z,t,c)
=
\epsilon_\theta(z,t,c)
+
\Delta_\phi(z,t,c),
\]
where \(z\) is a latent, \(t\) is a diffusion timestep, and \(c\) is the text conditioning. Only \(\phi\) is optimized. Unlike standard diffusion fine-tuning, our goal is not to improve noise prediction on noisy training samples. Instead, the adapter learns to map a cleaner latent to the noise prediction required to recover the preceding, noisier point of a trajectory generated by the frozen model.
\subsection{Trajectory-Supervised LoRA Inversion}
We construct the training set from deterministic DDIM sampling trajectories generated by the base model. We index each trajectory in the generation direction, so that \(z_i\) is the noisier latent at timestep \(t_i\) and \(z_{i+1}\) is the adjacent cleaner latent. For every transition, the frozen model produces the conditional noise prediction \(\epsilon_i^\star=\epsilon_\theta(z_i,t_i,c)\), followed by the deterministic update \(z_{i+1}=G_\theta(z_i,t_i,c)\). During inversion training, however, the LoRA model receives the cleaner endpoint \(z_{i+1}\) while retaining timestep \(t_i\). Its prediction is therefore \(\hat{\epsilon}_i=\epsilon_{\theta,\phi}(z_{i+1},t_i,c)\), and we regress it toward the noise prediction evaluated by the frozen model at the corresponding noisy endpoint:

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{inv}}=\mathbb{E}_{(z_i,z_{i+1},t_i,c)}\left[\left\|\epsilon_{\theta,\phi}(z_{i+1},t_i,c)-\epsilon_\theta(z_i,t_i,c)\right\|_2^2\right]. \]
This asymmetric input-target pair directly reflects the inference problem: inversion has access to \(z_{i+1}\), but must predict the direction that recovers \(z_i\). To obtain the predicted noisy endpoint, we apply a deterministic DDIM inverse transition to the LoRA prediction. Let \(\bar{\alpha}_i\) and \(\bar{\alpha}_{i+1}\) be the cumulative noise-schedule coefficients at the noisy and cleaner endpoints. The inverse update is

\[ \hat{x}_0=\frac{z_{i+1}-\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{i+1}}\,\hat{\epsilon}_i}{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{i+1}}}, \qquad \hat{z}_i=\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_i}\,\hat{x}_0+\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_i}\,\hat{\epsilon}_i. \]

We train by uniformly sampling adjacent transitions from the saved trajectories. Each training example contains the cleaner latent \(z_{i+1}\), timestep \(t_i\), prompt conditioning \(c\), and cached conditional target \(\epsilon_\theta(z_i,t_i,c)\).

\subsection{Cycle-Consistent Regularization}
Although \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{inv}}\) supplies a trajectory-derived supervision signal, a small noise-prediction error does not necessarily yield an inverse latent that is compatible with the frozen model's reverse dynamics. In particular, after inversion the base model evaluates its denoiser at \(\hat{z}_i\), rather than at the ground-truth trajectory point \(z_i\). Consequently, the prediction used during the subsequent generation step, \(\epsilon_\theta(\hat{z}_i,t_i,c)\), can differ from both the cached target and the LoRA prediction. We address this mismatch with a one-transition cycle-consistency objective. Starting from \(z_{i+1}\), we first predict \(\hat{z}_i=I_{\theta,\phi}(z_{i+1},t_i,c)\) using the LoRA-enabled inverse update. We then disable the adapter and map \(\hat{z}_i\) back toward the cleaner endpoint using one DDIM generation step of the frozen model, yielding \(\tilde{z}_{i+1}=G_\theta(\hat{z}_i,t_i,c)\). The cycle loss is

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cycle}}=\mathbb{E}_{(z_{i+1},t_i,c)}\left[\left\|G_\theta\!\left(I_{\theta,\phi}(z_{i+1},t_i,c),t_i,c\right)-z_{i+1}\right\|_2^2\right]. \]

The final objective combines trajectory-level noise supervision with compatibility under the frozen generation dynamics:

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{total}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{inv}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{cycle}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cycle}}, \]

where \(\lambda_{\mathrm{cycle}}\) controls the strength of the cycle regularizer. The parameters \(\theta\) remain frozen throughout training, including during the generation half of the cycle. We nevertheless retain gradients with respect to the input \(\hat{z}_i\), allowing the cycle error to propagate through the frozen denoiser and both DDIM updates to the LoRA parameters \(\phi\). Importantly, the generation step operates on the LoRA-predicted inverse latent rather than the saved ground-truth \(z_i\); the objective therefore penalizes the errors that the learned inversion actually introduces.

At inference time, the adapter is enabled only while constructing the inversion trajectory, whereas editing and reverse diffusion use the original frozen model. When combined with Direct Inversion, the LoRA trajectory replaces the conventional DDIM inversion trajectory, while the subsequent Direct Inversion correction and editor-specific generation procedure remain unchanged.

\section{Experiments}

\subsection{Inversion Quality}

We evaluate whether the learned inverse recovers a terminal latent with the statistical properties of the original Gaussian noise while retaining enough information to reconstruct the generated image. We use 1,522 held-out ReCap-COCO prompts and generate reference trajectories with Stable Diffusion XL at \(1024\times1024\) resolution, using deterministic DDIM sampling with 50 steps and classifier-free guidance scale \(w=1\). We compare standard DDIM inversion with LoRA ranks \(r\in\{8,16,32\}\), using \(\alpha\in\{4,8,16\}\), respectively. The adapter is enabled only during inversion and disabled during reconstruction, so the evaluation isolates the quality of the recovered terminal latent rather than modifying the reverse generation process.

Following \citet{staniszewski2026there}, we quantify latent normality using the mean of the 20 largest absolute Pearson correlation coefficients within \(8\times8\) latent patches and the KL divergence from a standard Gaussian distribution. Lower values indicate a more noise-like terminal latent. Reconstruction quality is measured between the original generated image and its reconstruction using MAE, LPIPS, PSNR, and SSIM. All tables report means without uncertainty intervals. Within each metric, we use a fixed numerical precision across rows.

\paragraph{Full-trajectory inversion.}
Table~\ref{tab:lora-rank-comparison} compares standard DDIM inversion with adapters enabled for all 50 inversion steps. Every LoRA rank improves both latent normality and image reconstruction, and performance generally increases with rank. The rank-32 adapter reduces correlation from \(0.1601\) to \(0.0972\) and KL divergence from \(0.8566\) to \(0.0504\), corresponding to relative reductions of \(39.3\%\) and \(94.1\%\), respectively. At the same time, it reduces reconstruction MAE by \(26.0\%\) and LPIPS by \(42.4\%\), increases PSNR from \(28.32\) to \(30.40\) dB, and increases SSIM from \(0.8112\) to \(0.8743\).

\paragraph{Error in plain and non-plain regions.}
Finally, we follow \citet{staniszewski2026there} and split each image into plain and non-plain regions. Plain regions contain little local variation and include areas such as skies, flat backgrounds, and smooth surfaces. Table~\ref{tab:plain-nonplain} reports the terminal-latent error relative to the original noise and the recovered latent's standard deviation in both regions. Standard DDIM inversion has higher error and lower variance in plain regions. LoRA reduces the error in both partitions and moves the standard deviation toward the unit variance of Gaussian noise. With full rank-32 LoRA, the plain-region error decreases from \(0.1956\) to \(0.0930\), while its standard deviation increases from \(0.8770\) to \(0.9479\).
